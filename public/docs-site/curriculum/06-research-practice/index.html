Phase 6 — Research practice: the frontier map, reading fluency, and producing novel work

Goal: convert competence into output. This phase has three parts: a survey of the remaining frontier topics (so no paper ambushes you), the reading workflow that makes "read any paper" true permanently, and the concrete path from replication to a result with your name on it.

Time: the survey is ~2 weeks; the rest is your ongoing practice.

6.1 Frontier survey — one paragraph each, enough to read anything

  • Mixture of Experts (MoE). Replace each MLP with E parallel MLPs ("experts") plus a tiny learned router that sends each *token* to its top-1/top-2 experts. Result: huge parameter count, small per-token compute ("671B params, 37B active" — DeepSeek-V3 — now parses exactly). Costs: all experts must sit in VRAM; balancing load across experts is the engineering headache. Read: Switch Transformer (2101.03961), Mixtral (2401.04088).
  • State-space models / Mamba (2312.00752). RNN-revival lineage: per-token state updated sequentially, O(1) memory per new token (no KV cache growth), trainable in parallel via clever math. Hybrids (a few attention layers + many SSM layers) appear in production models. Know the tradeoff: cheap long context vs. weaker exact recall.
  • Long context. Mostly: RoPE frequency rescaling tricks (YaRN, etc.) + continued training on long documents + ring attention to shard the sequence across GPUs + the KV-cache economics from Phase 4. "Needle in a haystack" is the (weak) standard eval.
  • Multimodality. The dominant recipe is gluing: a vision encoder — usually a ViT (2010.11929: split image into 16×16 patches, embed each patch like a token, run a transformer) trained with CLIP's contrastive objective (2103.00020: paired image/text encoders pushed to agree) — produces patch vectors; a small projector maps them into the LM's residual-stream space; the LM attends to them as if they were tokens. "Image tokens" are literally that. LLaVA (2304.08485) is the readable open version.
  • Agents & tool use. No new ML: the model emits a structured string ("tool call"), a *harness* (ordinary software — a while-loop with a parser) executes it and appends the result to the context, repeat. The research content is in post-training models to be good at this loop (RL over multi-turn trajectories) and in evals (SWE-bench-class). Your daily Claude Code session is this loop; you already understand agents better than most papers' related-work sections do.
  • RAG & embeddings. An embedding model = a transformer pooled to one vector per text, trained contrastively so related texts land close; retrieval = approximate nearest-neighbor over a vector store; RAG = paste the retrieved texts into the prompt. Engineering-heavy, research-light; know the vocabulary.
  • Distillation & synthetic data. Train small models on big models' outputs (sequence-level distillation — R1's distilled family) or on logits (classic, 1503.02531). Synthetic data: Self-Instruct (2212.10560), textbooks-style curricula (phi models), and the open worry of model collapse under recursive training.
  • Safety/alignment beyond RLHF (read these to speak Anthropic's language): scalable oversight (how do humans supervise superhuman outputs?), weak-to-strong generalization (2312.09390), AI debate, model organisms of misalignment — Sleeper Agents (2401.05566) and Alignment Faking (2412.14093) are the must-reads, both Anthropic, both readable now.

6.2 Reading fluency: the workflow

Permanent fluency = vocabulary (you now have it) + a triage habit:

  • Three-pass method (Keshav): pass 1 — title/abstract/figures/conclusion, 5 min, decide if it matters; pass 2 — full read, skip proofs, 30 min; pass 3 — re-derive/re-implement (reserve for ~1 paper/month). Most papers die honorably at pass 1.
  • Figures first, always. In ML papers the figures + tables are the paper; prose is mostly justification. Ask: what's the x-axis, what's the baseline, *what would falsify this claim*, and "would this survive the checkpoint questions of Phases 2–4?" (compute math, contamination, judge bias).
  • Diet: ~10 abstracts/day (arXiv cs.CL/cs.LG via a filter, or alphaXiv/HF daily-papers for socially-ranked feeds), 2 papers/week at pass 2, 1/month at pass 3. Sources worth following directly: transformer-circuits.pub, Anthropic/OpenAI/DeepMind research blogs, the Alignment Forum, EleutherAI Discord, Sasha Rush & Andrej Karpathy & Neel Nanda in video form.
  • Keep the vocabulary notebook going; it's your personal Lingua Latina glossary, and in six months it will be the curriculum you hand someone else.

6.3 From reader to producer: the ladder

What a research engineer at a lab actually does all day: write experiment code on a shared codebase, launch and babysit runs, make plots that answer one question cleanly, write short docs, review others' experiments. Your 15 years of SWE is ~70% of the job. The missing 30% is *experimental taste*: smallest experiment that answers the question, ruthless baselines, error bars, "what else could explain this?" That is trained by the ladder:

  1. Replicate a result (Phase 5 builds started this). Verbatim replication teaches; published replication *with a discrepancy found* is already a contribution.
  2. Replicate with a twist: change one variable — different model family, data domain, scale. "Does X hold under Y?" is the highest-acceptance-rate genre in research.
  3. Ablate: take a method with five components, find the two doing the work. Genuinely valued, chronically undersupplied.
  4. Original question. By now problems will be falling out of your replication notes — every "huh, that's weird" is a candidate.

Problems an individual can win: small-model science (your scaling-law rig), interp (Neel Nanda's 200-problems list is a literal menu), evals with rigorous methodology (your lane — see below), post-training recipe science at ≤3B scale, data-curation experiments. Problems to avoid: frontier pretraining, capability records, anything whose answer costs >$5k of compute to even ask. Honest budget reality: $500 covers a serious interp or eval project; $3–5k covers a post-training ablation study at 1–3B scale.

6.4 Publishing without a lab affiliation

The 2026 path, in escalating formality — note that several canonical results in this curriculum (logit lens; much of the circuits agenda) debuted as *blog posts*:

  1. Blog post / Alignment Forum or LessWrong post with a clean reproducible repo. The interp and alignment communities treat these as first-class literature; lab researchers actually read them.
  2. arXiv preprint (needs a one-time endorsement for your first cs.LG submission — ask anyone who's published there; this is normal and easy).
  3. Workshop paper at NeurIPS/ICLR/ICML workshops — friendlier review than main tracks, real visibility, deadlines that create useful pressure.
  4. Open-source artifacts count double: TinyZero, open-r1, nanoGPT-speedrun leaderboard entries — repos that became citation magnets and job offers. Anthropic and OpenAI demonstrably hire from exactly this portfolio shape (independent blog-post researchers becoming staff is a repeating pattern, not an exception).

Cold truth that should encourage you: the bar for "useful contribution" is lower than it looks from outside — most published work is replication-with-a-twist or ablation — while the bar for *rigor* is where outsiders actually fail (no baselines, no error bars, contaminated evals). Your Phase 2–4 checkpoints are precisely the rigor muscles.

6.5 Capstone menu (pick one, 4–8 weeks)

Each is sized for one person, includes success criteria, and lands in a publishable artifact:

  1. The eval you're uniquely positioned to build. Take the Gutenberg implicit-knowledge benchmark experience — human-gold protocols, verifier-anchoring pitfalls, recall auditing — and either (a) finish it to publication grade with a human baseline and an error taxonomy, or (b) write the *methodology* paper/post: "how benchmark verification fails," demonstrated across 2–3 public benchmarks. Success: a result a lab evals team would cite. Recommended: you have a 12-month head start on this one.
  2. Interp: train SAEs across your Phase-2 model's training checkpoints; when do features form, and does feature formation align with the induction-head phase change? Success: one clean figure nobody has made at your exact setting + a replication-grade repo.
  3. Post-training science: at 0.5–1.5B scale, a controlled DPO vs. GRPO comparison under matched data/compute, with reward-hacking and length-bias measurements. Success: an ablation table people screenshot.
  4. Small-model R1-science: reproduce R1-Zero-style training (TinyZero-style) and study *one* phenomenon rigorously — e.g., when does the length-inflation start, and is it reward hacking or genuine reasoning? Success: a causal answer, not a vibe.
  5. Data: your Phase-2 scaling rig + two data mixtures (e.g., FineWeb vs FineWeb-Edu) → "data quality buys you X× compute at this scale," with fitted curves. Success: a number with error bars.

6.6 Exit criteria for the whole curriculum

  • You pick up any new arXiv paper in this space and pass-1 it in five minutes, locating it on the map (pretraining / post-training / inference / interp / evals) and naming its nearest ancestor in paper-ladder.md.
  • You have one published artifact (post + repo at minimum) that a stranger has starred, cited, or argued with.
  • The "what actually IS that?" question has a reflexive answer for every term you use: file, function, loop, or number.

When both goals feel routine rather than aspirational, the curriculum is done — and your vocabulary notebook is the proof it happened.
