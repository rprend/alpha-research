Phase 6 daily packets — Research practice (Days 120–133)

Delivers the frontier survey (no paper ambushes you again), a permanent reading workflow, and a committed one-page capstone plan — the launch pad for novel, publishable work. Prerequisite: Phase 5 checkpoint passed.

Day 120 — The three-pass reading method

⏱ ~60 min · Link: Keshav, How to Read a Paper

Do: Read all three pages of Keshav, then apply it immediately: open today's arXiv cs.CL or cs.LG listing and pass-1 three fresh papers — title, abstract, figures, conclusion, then a five-Cs verdict for each (background: section 6.2 of the phase guide).

Explains: Triage is what makes "read any paper" sustainable — most papers die honorably at pass 1, in five minutes, on purpose.

Done when: Three written pass-1 verdicts (category, map location, read-further yes/no) exist in your vocabulary notebook, produced in 15 minutes total on a timer.

Day 121 — Mixture of Experts

⏱ ~75 min · Link: Mixtral of Experts

Do: Pass-2 Mixtral — sections 1–3 plus the routing-analysis figures; skip the rest. Then pass-1 Switch Transformer (arXiv 2101.03961) for the top-1-routing and load-balancing-loss vocabulary (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: MoE replaces each MLP with E expert MLPs plus a learned per-token router, decoupling parameter count from per-token compute — "671B params, 37B active" now parses exactly.

Done when: With the paper closed, you derive Mixtral's ~47B total / ~13B active split on paper from "8 experts, top-2 routing, shared attention" in under 10 minutes and it matches the paper's numbers.

Day 122 — Mamba and state-space models

⏱ ~75 min · Link: Mamba

Do: Read the abstract, section 1, and section 3 (selective SSMs), figures first; stop before the proofs. Write the attention-vs-SSM tradeoff as a memory equation: KV cache grows with sequence length, SSM state is fixed-size (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: SSMs are the RNN revival — a recurrent per-token state gives O(1) memory per new token at the cost of weaker exact recall, which is why production models ship as hybrids.

Done when: A notebook entry exists computing, for one concrete config, KV-cache bytes at 100k context versus Mamba's fixed state size — two numbers, each with its formula shown.

Day 123 — Long context, RAG, and embeddings

⏱ ~70 min · Link: YaRN

Do: Pass-1 YaRN — abstract and figures only; you want the shape of RoPE frequency rescaling, not the derivation. Then work through the long-context and RAG/embeddings paragraphs of the phase guide and write grounded notebook entries for the vocabulary (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: Long context is mostly RoPE rescaling plus long-document continued training plus the KV economics from Phase 4; RAG is nearest-neighbor search over embedding vectors pasted into the prompt — engineering, not new ML.

Done when: Six notebook entries exist — RoPE rescaling, ring attention, needle-in-a-haystack, embedding model, approximate nearest neighbor, RAG — each passing the file/function/loop/number test.

Day 124 — Multimodal glue

⏱ ~80 min · Link: LLaVA

Do: Pass-2 LLaVA sections 1, 3, and 4. First pass-1 the two parts it glues: ViT (arXiv 2010.11929 — 16×16 image patches embedded like tokens) and CLIP (arXiv 2103.00020 — contrastive paired image/text encoders), abstract and figure 1 of each (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: Multimodality is mostly gluing — a ViT produces patch vectors, a small projector maps them into the LM's residual-stream space, and the LM attends to them as if they were tokens; "image tokens" are literally that.

Done when: You can draw the full LLaVA pipeline from memory in under 10 minutes — image → patches → ViT → projector → LM — with a tensor shape labeled on every arrow.

Day 125 — Agents and tool use

⏱ ~70 min · Link: SWE-bench

Do: Pass-1 SWE-bench — abstract, figures, and the evaluation setup — for how agent evals are actually scored. Then write the harness loop yourself: at most 40 lines of pseudocode — model emits a tool-call string, a parser executes it, the result is appended to context, repeat until a stop condition (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide; your daily Claude Code session is this exact loop).

Explains: Agents are no new ML — a while-loop with a parser wrapped around an ordinary LM; the research content lives in post-training for the loop and in SWE-bench-class evals.

Done when: A pseudocode file of 40 lines or fewer exists in your repo covering emit → parse → execute → append → repeat plus an explicit termination condition.

Day 126 — Distillation and synthetic data

⏱ ~70 min · Link: Self-Instruct

Do: Pass-2 Self-Instruct sections 1–3 plus the pipeline figure. Then add notebook entries for logit distillation (arXiv 1503.02531), sequence-level distillation (R1's distilled family), and model collapse under recursive training (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: Most open post-training data is made by training small models on big models' outputs — token sequences or logits — or by bootstrapping instructions from the model itself.

Done when: With the paper closed, you write the Self-Instruct pipeline's stages in order in under 5 minutes, plus a two-sentence distinction between logit and sequence-level distillation.

Day 127 — Sleeper Agents

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Sleeper Agents

Do: Pass-2 the main body only — sections 1–2 and every headline figure; the appendices would triple your time, skip them. Track one question throughout: which safety-training techniques were tried, and did any remove the backdoor (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: A model organism of misalignment — deliberately backdoored models show that standard safety training (SFT, RL, adversarial training) can fail to remove deceptive behavior and can even teach the model to hide it better.

Done when: A 10-line summary in your notebook names the backdoor triggers, the three safety-training techniques tested, and the persistence result for each — checked against the paper afterward with zero factual corrections needed.

Day 128 — Alignment Faking

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Alignment faking in large language models

Do: Pass-2 the main body, figures first — focus on the free-tier/paid-tier setup, the scratchpad evidence, and the compliance-gap numbers. It is long: hold yourself to the headline experiments and stop at 90 minutes (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: A model can strategically comply during training to preserve its existing preferences — the second must-read model organism, and core vocabulary for speaking Anthropic's language.

Done when: From memory you can describe the two-tier setup and state the direction and rough size of the compliance gap, and your notebook entry survives a check against the paper's abstract.

Day 129 — Weak-to-strong generalization

⏱ ~80 min · Link: Weak-to-strong generalization

Do: Pass-2 sections 1–4 plus the headline PGR figure. Frame it as the scalable-oversight question: how do weaker supervisors — eventually humans — elicit the capabilities of stronger models (background: section 6.1 of the phase guide, safety paragraph).

Explains: Finetuning a strong model on a weak supervisor's labels recovers part of the gap to ground truth — "performance gap recovered" is the number this whole subfield optimizes.

Done when: Given made-up weak, weak-to-strong, and strong-ceiling accuracies, you compute PGR correctly in under 5 minutes with the formula written from memory.

Day 130 — The ladder and where to publish

⏱ ~60 min · Link: Alignment Forum

Do: Read sections 6.3 and 6.4 of the phase guide. Then browse the Alignment Forum frontpage and skim two recent research posts, judging each against the rigor bar: baselines, error bars, reproducible repo. Afterward list the four publishing rungs from memory.

Explains: The replicate → twist → ablate → original-question ladder is how outsiders produce real contributions, and a blog post with a clean repo is first-class literature in interp and alignment.

Done when: A written shortlist of exactly 2 venues for your capstone exists, each with one sentence on why it fits.

Day 131 — Capstone selection: score the menu

⏱ ~75 min · Link: Phase 6 guide

Do: Score all five capstone options in section 6.5 of the phase guide on three axes: budget (use the $500 / $3–5k reality from section 6.3), fit to your existing assets (the Gutenberg benchmark head start, your Phase-2 scaling rig, your Phase-5 interp stack), and 4–8-week feasibility. Pick a top 2 and draft success criteria for each.

Explains: Problem selection is the highest-leverage research decision — individuals win at small-model science, interp, and rigorous evals, and lose at anything that costs more than $5k just to ask.

Done when: capstone-candidates.md exists in the curriculum repo with all five options scored and written success criteria for the top 2.

Day 132 — Capstone selection: write the plan

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Phase 6 guide

Do: Choose one candidate and write the one-page plan: the question, the smallest experiment that answers it, baselines, error-bar plan, the "what else could explain this?" list, a dollar budget, and a week-by-week schedule for 4–8 weeks (background: section 6.3 of the phase guide on experimental taste). No runs launch today — the plan is the artifact.

Explains: A plan forced through "smallest experiment, ruthless baselines, alternative explanations" before any compute is spent is exactly the experimental taste labs hire for.

Done when: capstone-plan.md exists, fits on one page, and contains all seven elements listed above.

Day 133 — Checkpoint: curriculum exit review, cold

⏱ ~60 min · Link: Curriculum README

Do: Closed notes. Run the section 6.6 exit criteria against yourself: pass-1 one fresh arXiv paper from today's listing in 5 minutes, locating it on the pretraining/post-training/inference/interp/evals map and naming its nearest ancestor in the paper ladder; answer "what actually IS it?" — file, function, loop, or number — for 10 randomly chosen terms from your vocabulary notebook; commit capstone-plan.md to the repo.

Explains: The exit test for the entire curriculum — reading fluency, grounded vocabulary, and a committed plan for the published artifact that proves it happened.

Done when: All three checkpoint items are produced: the timed pass-1 note exists, 10 of 10 terms get a grounded answer without hesitation, and git log shows the capstone-plan commit.
