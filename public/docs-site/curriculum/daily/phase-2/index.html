Phase 2 daily packets — Training at scale (Days 36–56)

This phase pretrains your Phase-1 GPT for real — 124M params on ~10B tokens of web data — and installs the field's cost arithmetic (6ND, MFU, memory ledgers, scaling laws). Prerequisite: Phase 1 checkpoint passed cold.

Day 36 — What pretraining data actually is

⏱ ~75 min · Link: FineWeb blog post

Do: Read the FineWeb blog post end to end, skimming the ablation details: focus on text extraction, the heuristic filters, MinHash deduplication, and the FineWeb-Edu classifier (background: section 2.1 of the phase guide).

Explains: "Trained on the internet" means a concrete batch pipeline — Common Crawl WARC files → extracted text → language ID → quality filters → dedup → parquet files of {text, url, metadata}.

Done when: A five-line pipeline summary (WARC → text → lang-ID → filters → dedup → classifier), one sentence per stage, exists in your vocabulary notebook, written without looking back at the post.

Day 37 — Build your training data: tokens on disk

⏱ ~90 min · Link: karpathy/llm.c

Do: Using llm.c's dev/data/fineweb.py as the reference, download the FineWeb-Edu 10BT sample and tokenize it with GPT-2 BPE into flat uint16 .bin shards on your laptop (free, CPU; leave it running). Stop once ≥2B tokens are on disk — you'll regenerate the full set on the rented node later (background: section 2.1 of the phase guide, point 3).

Explains: A pretraining dataset on disk is literally one giant flat array of uint16 token ids; a "batch" is random offsets into it, each reading seq_len+1 consecutive tokens.

Done when: Shard files totaling ≥2B tokens (≥4 GB) exist, and a 5-line script memmaps one shard, prints the first 32 token ids, and decodes them back to legible text.

Day 38 — 6ND napkin math

⏱ ~60 min · Link: Phase 2 guide

Do: Derive FLOPs ≈ 6ND on paper (forward = 2ND, backward = 2× forward), then compute FLOPs, wall-clock, and dollar cost for three runs: your 124M × 10B on 8×A100 at 40% MFU, GPT-3 (175B × 300B), and Llama-3-70B (70B × 15T) (background: section 2.2 of the phase guide).

Explains: One formula — 6 × params × tokens — lets you sanity-check any "we trained X" claim on a napkin, from your $25 run to a $100M frontier run.

Done when: A one-page napkin sheet exists with all three estimates, and your 124M estimate lands in the 40–90 minute wall-clock range.

Day 39 — Upgrade 1: char tokenizer → BPE

⏱ ~90 min · Link: openai/tiktoken

Do: Swap the char-level tokenizer in your Phase-1 GPT for GPT-2 BPE via tiktoken: vocab 50257, resize the embedding and unembedding matrices, and retrain briefly on Shakespeare as a sanity check (background: section 2.7 of the phase guide, build step 1).

Explains: Your model now consumes the same token ids as the .bin shards from Day 37 — the tokenizer is the contract between data on disk and the embedding table.

Done when: A 5-minute Shakespeare run produces samples that decode through tiktoken into real English words.

Day 40 — Upgrade 2: bf16 mixed precision

⏱ ~75 min · Link: karpathy/nanoGPT

Do: Add bf16 autocast to your training loop (fp32 master weights, bf16 matmuls), diffing against nanoGPT's train.py; benchmark tokens/sec and peak VRAM versus your fp32 baseline on a GPU — rent a 4090 if you have none locally, expected cost under $2 (background: section 2.4 of the phase guide).

Explains: bf16 keeps fp32's exponent range with a chopped mantissa — half the memory and ~2× tensor-core speed with no overflow drama, which is why it beat fp16.

Done when: A two-row table (fp32 vs bf16) of tokens/sec and peak VRAM exists in your repo, with bf16 at least 1.5× faster.

Day 41 — Upgrades 3+4: grad accumulation and torch.compile

⏱ ~90 min · Link: karpathy/nanoGPT

Do: Add gradient accumulation (sum grads over micro-batches, one optimizer step) and torch.compile to your trainer, diffing against nanoGPT; extend the Day-40 benchmark table; same rented 4090, expected cost under $2 (background: section 2.4 of the phase guide).

Explains: Accumulation makes batch size independent of VRAM (mathematically identical to one big batch); torch.compile fuses your Python ops into fewer GPU kernels for a free ~1.3–2×.

Done when: The benchmark table has four rows, compiled-bf16 tokens/sec is ≥2× the fp32 eager baseline, and a 200-step accumulated run matches the equivalent big-batch run's loss within 0.02.

Day 42 — FlashAttention: memory movement is the bottleneck

⏱ ~90 min · Link: FlashAttention (arXiv 2205.14135)

Do: Read sections 1–3 and figure 1; skip the appendix proofs. Track one question throughout: where does the (T, T) score matrix live at each step (background: section 2.4 of the phase guide)?

Explains: GPU performance is usually bound by memory movement, not arithmetic — FlashAttention is the same attention math tiled through fast on-chip SRAM so the score matrix never touches slow HBM: exact outputs, 2–4× faster, O(T) memory.

Done when: Closed notes, in under 5 minutes, you can sketch the HBM-vs-SRAM tiling diagram and state why the (T, T) matrix is never materialized in HBM.

Day 43 — Memory ledger and the knobs that matter

⏱ ~75 min · Link: Phase 2 guide

Do: Write the mixed-precision AdamW memory ledger from memory, then verify against section 2.4 of the phase guide; then implement the standard recipe in your trainer: AdamW(0.9, 0.95, wd=0.1), linear warmup → cosine decay LR schedule, gradient clipping at norm 1.0; plot the schedule (background: section 2.6 of the phase guide).

Explains: Training a model takes ~16 bytes per parameter (2 bf16 weight + 2 grad + 4 fp32 master + 8 Adam) before activations — the number that dictates all sharding — and peak LR is the single most important hyperparameter.

Done when: An LR-schedule plot exists in your repo and you can reproduce the 2+2+4+8 = 16 bytes/param breakdown from memory.

Day 44 — Four ways to split the work

⏱ ~75 min · Link: Megatron-LM (arXiv 1909.08053)

Do: Skim Megatron-LM sections 1–3 for the tensor-parallelism diagrams (how one matmul is sliced across GPUs), then write your own one-paragraph grounded definition of each of DDP, ZeRO/FSDP, tensor parallel, and pipeline parallel (background: section 2.5 of the phase guide).

Explains: All distributed training is four composable answers to "what do we split?" — batches, optimizer state shards, weight-matrix slices, or layers — glued together by collective ops like all-reduce.

Done when: A four-entry section exists in your vocabulary notebook; each entry names what is split and what gets communicated between GPUs.

Day 45 — DDP hands-on

⏱ ~75 min · Link: karpathy/nanoGPT

Do: Run your trainer under torchrun with 2+ processes, following nanoGPT's DDP setup — either a rented 2-GPU machine (under $3) or the gloo backend on your laptop's CPU (free). Print a gradient norm from each rank before and after the all-reduce to watch them converge to the same value (background: section 2.5 of the phase guide, point 1).

Explains: DDP is data parallelism made concrete: every rank holds a full model copy, sees different batches, and an all-reduce (elementwise sum across ranks) of gradients keeps every copy stepping identically.

Done when: A 2-process torchrun completes 100 steps and a per-rank checksum of the weights printed at the end is identical across ranks.

Day 46 — Launch prep and smoke run

⏱ ~75 min · Link: karpathy/llm.c

Do: Pick your launch vehicle (llm.c's GPT-2 124M reproduction or nanoGPT's reproduce-GPT-2 config) and write a launch checklist into your repo: data on node, tmux, checkpoint-every-N-steps to persistent disk, logging, kill switch, predicted wall-clock and cost from Day 38. Then run the exact config scaled down for 10 minutes on one cheap GPU as a smoke test, expected cost under $1.

Explains: A big training run is an operations problem — the checklist-and-smoke-test habit is what separates a $25 run from a $80 run that died at 90% with no checkpoints.

Done when: The checklist file exists in your repo and the smoke run produced a checkpoint file that loads and resumes.

Day 47 — THE pretraining run: launch

⏱ ~90 min · Link: karpathy/nanoGPT

Do: Rent an 8×A100 node (Lambda/RunPod) and launch the 124M-param run on ~10B FineWeb-Edu tokens, expected cost $20–80; within the first 15 minutes verify loss is falling from ~10.8 (ln 50257) and tokens/sec implies 30–45% MFU, then walk away — do not babysit (background: section 2.7 of the phase guide, build step 1; budget alternative: 30–50M params on 2B tokens, one 4090, overnight, under $10).

Explains: This is the same for-loop as your laptop runs — bigger N, bigger D, more machines — and the launch discipline (verify, then leave) is the actual job.

Done when: The run has passed 10% of total steps with train loss below 6.0 and at least one checkpoint file written to persistent disk.

Day 48 — Monitor, sample, reconcile, terminate

⏱ ~75 min · Link: karpathy/nanoGPT

Do: As the run finishes, sample ~200 tokens from an early, middle, and final checkpoint with the same prompt (nanoGPT's sample.py); reconcile observed wall-clock and nvidia-smi utilization against your Day-38 prediction and write down the ratio; then terminate the node — the meter is the lesson (background: section 2.7 of the phase guide, build step 3).

Explains: Watching checkpoints go gibberish → grammar → facts is what "knowledge crystallizing into the weights" literally looks like, and prediction-vs-observed reconciliation is the core research-engineer habit.

Done when: A samples.md with the three checkpoint generations, final val loss, and the predicted-vs-observed wall-clock ratio exists in your repo, and the cloud node is terminated.

Day 49 — Kaplan: scaling laws

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Kaplan et al. 2020 (arXiv 2001.08361)

Do: Read sections 1–3 closely and skim the rest; stare at figure 1 until the claim lands: loss vs. N, D, and compute are straight lines on log-log axes across many orders of magnitude (background: section 2.3 of the phase guide). You replicate this in Days 50–53.

Explains: Loss falls as a smooth power law in N, D, and compute — the predictability that let labs extrapolate a 100× bigger run's loss before spending the money, and the reason anyone bet billions.

Done when: A half-page closed-book summary exists in your notebook containing the power-law form L(C) = (C₀/C)^α and the three quantities Kaplan varies.

Day 50 — Mini scaling study 1: the small rungs

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Phase 2 guide

Do: Define a 4-model ladder (≈3M/10M/30M/85M params) at fixed D/N = 20, then train the three smallest on FineWeb-Edu on a rented 4090, expected cost ~$3; log N, D, 6ND FLOPs, and final val loss for each (background: section 2.7 of the phase guide, build step 2).

Explains: This is the literal methodology of the Kaplan and Chinchilla papers — train a ladder, measure final loss — at 1/10⁹ the budget.

Done when: A results.csv exists with three complete rows: N, D, FLOPs, final val loss.

Day 51 — Mini scaling study 2: top rung and first fit

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Phase 2 guide

Do: Launch the 85M run (≈1.7B tokens; several hours on a 4090 — start it and leave it, expected cost ~$5), then write the fitting script: linear regression of log(loss) on log(FLOPs), and produce a preliminary log-log plot from the three finished points.

Explains: Fitting a power law is just a straight line in log-log space — two parameters that summarize your entire ladder and let you extrapolate beyond it.

Done when: The 85M run is launched and a log-log plot with three points and a fitted line exists in your repo.

Day 52 — Chinchilla: the corrected recipe

⏱ ~75 min · Link: Chinchilla (arXiv 2203.15556)

Do: Read sections 1–3, focusing on the three estimation approaches and the D ≈ 20N conclusion; then compute where your Day-47 run sits (10B tokens / 124M params ≈ 80× — overtrained 4×) and write down why Llama-3-8B at D/N ≈ 1875 is still rational (background: section 2.3 of the phase guide).

Explains: For fixed training compute, loss-optimal allocation is ~20 tokens per parameter — and labs deliberately violate it because a smaller model that is cheap to serve a billion times is worth extra training cost.

Done when: A notebook entry exists stating, with the numbers, whether your run was over- or under-trained per Chinchilla and the training-vs-serving-compute reason practice diverged.

Day 53 — Mini scaling study 3: predict, then verify

⏱ ~90 min · Link: Phase 2 guide

Do: Add the finished 85M point and refit; pick a 5th size NOT on your ladder (a held-out ~50M, or ~160M if you'll spend ~$10 extrapolating), write down its predicted loss and a confidence band BEFORE training, then train it (rented GPU, expected cost $3–10) and compare (background: section 2.7 of the phase guide, build step 2).

Explains: Predict-then-verify is the whole point of scaling laws — and your first genuinely research-shaped artifact: a quantitative prediction made before the experiment, then tested.

Done when: The log-log plot shows four fitted points, the prediction marker, and the 5th measured point, and the prediction error is recorded in results.csv.

Day 54 — GPT-3: in-context learning

⏱ ~90 min · Link: GPT-3 (arXiv 2005.14165)

Do: Read sections 1–3 (it's 75 pages — skim everything after), focusing on figure 1.2 and how zero-/one-/few-shot are defined: conditioning only, no gradient updates (background: section 2.8 of the phase guide). Sanity-check their compute with 6ND as you read.

Explains: Few-shot prompting emerged from scale alone — no fine-tuning — which is the discovery that created the prompting era.
