Phase 2 — Training at scale: data, FLOPs, and many GPUs

Goal: pretrain a real (small) language model on real web data with rented GPUs, and acquire the *cost arithmetic* of the field — so that when a paper says "trained on 15T tokens on 16k H100s," you can sanity-check it on a napkin. This phase is where "lab-scale" papers become readable.

Time: 3–4 weeks. Cost: $30–100 of rented GPU time.

2.1 What pretraining data actually is

"Trained on the internet" means, concretely:

  1. Common Crawl — a nonprofit that scrapes the web and publishes ~90 TB/month of raw HTML in WARC files on S3. This is the upstream source of essentially every open pretraining dataset.
  2. A filtering pipeline (a big batch job): extract text from HTML → language-ID → heuristic quality filters (punctuation ratios, boilerplate removal) → deduplication (hash-based, e.g. MinHash) → often a *model-based* quality score (a small classifier trained to recognize "educational" text). The output: parquet files of {text, url, metadata}.
  3. Tokenize once, store as one giant flat array of uint16 token-ids on disk (nanoGPT literally produces train.bin). A training "batch" is batch_size random offsets into this array, each reading seq_len + 1 consecutive tokens (the +1: inputs are tokens 0..T-1, targets are tokens 1..T — same array shifted by one).

The open-source exemplar: FineWeb / FineWeb-Edu (Hugging Face, 2024) — read their blog post; it is the best public documentation of what data work actually involves, and "data work" is most of what pretraining teams do. The deepest under-appreciated truth of the field: *architecture is mostly settled; data quality and quantity are where runs are won*.

2.2 The cost model: 6ND (memorize this)

Training FLOPs ≈ 6 × N × D — N = parameter count, D = training tokens. (Forward pass ≈ 2ND — each token "touches" each weight in one multiply-accumulate = 2 FLOPs; backward ≈ 2× forward; total 6.) This one formula unlocks every "we trained X" paper:

  • An H100 does ~1×10¹⁵ bf16 FLOP/s peak; real training achieves 30–45% of that (MFU, model FLOPs utilization — actual useful FLOPs over peak).
  • Your run this phase: GPT-2-small-class, N=124M, D=10B tokens → 7.4×10¹⁸ FLOPs → at ~3×10¹⁵ useful FLOP/s on a rented 8×A100 node, ~40–90 minutes, ~$20–40. (Karpathy's llm.c reproduced GPT-2 124M for ~$20.)
  • GPT-3 (2020): N=175B, D=300B → ~3×10²³ FLOPs. Llama-3-70B: 70B × 15T → ~6×10²⁴. Frontier runs as of 2025–26: ~10²⁶, months on tens of thousands of GPUs, $100M+. Same for-loop as your laptop. Bigger N, bigger D, more machines.

2.3 Scaling laws: the field's load-bearing empirical fact

Kaplan et al. 2020 (arXiv:2001.08361): loss falls as a smooth power law in N, D, and compute, over many orders of magnitude. Concretely: train a ladder of model sizes, plot final loss vs. compute on log-log axes, get a straight line — *which lets you extrapolate what a 100× bigger run will achieve before spending the money*. This predictability, not any single architecture trick, is why labs were willing to bet billions. (GPT-4's report shows loss predicted accurately from runs 1000× smaller.)

Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022, arXiv:2203.15556) corrected Kaplan's recipe: for a fixed compute budget, the loss-optimal allocation is roughly D ≈ 20 × N (GPT-3 was badly undertrained at D/N ≈ 1.7). Then practice diverged from theory again: Llama-3-8B trained at D/N ≈ 1875 — wildly "over-trained" — because Chinchilla optimizes *training* compute only, and a smaller model that's cheap to *serve* a billion times is worth extra training cost. Know both numbers and why they differ; it's a favorite interview-style probe of real understanding.

2.4 Making one GPU fast and not OOM

The memory ledger for training, per parameter, mixed-precision AdamW: bf16 weight (2) + bf16 grad (2) + fp32 master weight (4) + two fp32 Adam buffers (8) = ~16 bytes/param — before activations. So "training a 7B model" needs ~112 GB *just for states*: more than any single GPU. Hence everything below.

  • Mixed precision (bf16): do matmuls in 16-bit (half the memory, ~2× speed on tensor cores), keep an fp32 master copy of weights for stable accumulation. bf16 = same exponent range as fp32 with chopped mantissa, which is why it won over fp16 (no overflow drama).
  • Gradient accumulation: can't fit batch 512? Run 16 micro-batches of 32, summing grads, then one optimizer step. Mathematically identical.
  • Activation checkpointing: don't store intermediate activations for backward; recompute them. Trades ~30% compute for huge memory savings.
  • FlashAttention (Dao 2022, arXiv:2205.14135): the same attention math reorganized so the (T, T) score matrix is never materialized in slow GPU main memory — computed tile-by-tile in fast on-chip SRAM. Exact same outputs, 2–4× faster, O(T) memory. Read it as your introduction to the systems insight that GPU performance is usually bound by memory movement, not arithmetic — the single most important systems fact in the field (it returns in Phase 4 as the reason inference is slow).
  • torch.compile: JIT-fuses your Python ops into fewer kernels. Free ~1.3–2×.

2.5 Many GPUs: four ways to split the work

All distributed training is four composable answers to "what do we split?" — and the communication between GPUs is just collective array operations (an all-reduce = "every GPU ends up with the elementwise sum of everyone's array") over NVLink/InfiniBand:

  1. Data parallel (DDP): every GPU holds a full model copy, sees different batches; after backward, all-reduce (average) the gradients so every copy steps identically. Simplest; works until the model doesn't fit on one GPU.
  2. ZeRO / FSDP: still data parallel, but *shard* the optimizer states, grads, and even params across GPUs, gathering shards just-in-time. Same math, fits much bigger models.
  3. Tensor parallel: split individual weight matrices across GPUs (each computes a slice of every matmul). Needs fast interconnect → used *within* a node of 8.
  4. Pipeline parallel: put layers 1–20 on one node, 21–40 on the next; micro-batches flow assembly-line style.

Frontier runs compose all of these ("4D parallelism"). Operationally, a big run = a slurm/k8s job of thousands of processes, checkpointing every N minutes, with on-call engineers restarting it around hardware failures and the occasional mysterious loss spike. Reading the logistics sections of the Llama-3 or OPT papers (OPT's published *logbook* is a classic) tells you what research-engineer work at a lab actually is — far more of it is this than is inventing architectures.

2.6 The knobs that matter (hyperparameters)

  • AdamW (β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95, wd=0.1) — the default everywhere; "W" = weight decay applied directly to weights rather than through the gradient.
  • LR schedule: linear warmup (~1–2% of steps; Adam's variance estimates are garbage at step 0) then cosine decay to ~10% of peak. Peak LR is *the* most important hyperparameter; too high → loss spikes/divergence.
  • Gradient clipping at norm 1.0: if the global gradient vector is longer than 1, rescale it — a seatbelt against bad batches.
  • Batch size in tokens (e.g. "0.5M tokens/batch"); bigger = smoother gradients but diminishing returns.
  • (Aware-of, not study: muP — a parameterization making optimal LR transfer from small to large models, so you tune on cheap runs.)

2.7 The build

  1. Pretrain your model for real. Take your Phase-1 nanoGPT, swap char-tokenizer → BPE (yours or tiktoken), and train a 124M-param model on ~10B tokens of FineWeb-Edu sample on a rented 8×A100/H100 node (Lambda/RunPod, $20–80). Karpathy's nanoGPT "reproduce GPT-2" instructions or llm.c are your reference. Watch the loss; sample from checkpoints as it trains — see knowledge crystallize from gibberish to grammar to facts. *(Budget alternative: 30–50M params on 2B tokens, one 4090, overnight, <$10.)*
  2. Run a mini scaling-law study — your first genuinely research-shaped artifact: train 4 sizes (e.g. 3M/10M/30M/85M) at fixed D/N, plot final loss vs. compute log-log, fit the power law, predict a 5th model's loss before training it, then train it and check. This is the actual methodology of the Kaplan/Chinchilla papers at 1/10⁹ the budget.
  3. Compute the memory ledger and 6ND estimate for your runs *before* launching; reconcile with observed wall-clock and nvidia-smi. The reconciliation habit is the skill.
